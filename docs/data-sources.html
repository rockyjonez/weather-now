Where the weather dashboard gets its data

Every source is a free public service. None of them needs an API key, a licence or an account, so the whole dashboard can be rebuilt or forked without asking anyone for access. It works for any address in the United States, Canada, Mexico or Brazil.

Two ways a source reaches the page. Most are called straight from the browser, so the data is as fresh as the provider makes it. Three are fetched by a scheduled job on our server first, because those providers limit how often an unregistered caller may ask. Every viewer then shares one copy.

The direct and cached tags in the last column show which is which.

Brazil, Canada and Mexico. Type a CEP such as 01310-100, a Canadian or Mexican postcode, a city name or a coordinate pair. Everything on the page then works from that point: forecast, hourly rows, 10-day outlook, health scores, radar, satellite, fire detections, sky and launches.

Brazil gets real storm warnings. INMET publishes its warnings as map polygons, and the page tests whether your point falls inside each one, the same way it handles American alerts. Warnings appear in the banner, the alerts tab and the relevant weather tabs, with INMET's own risk text and safety instructions.

One thing is still United States only. The National Weather Service forecast is replaced by Open-Meteo, which is a computer model rather than a meteorologist's forecast. Canada and Mexico still link out to their agencies, because neither publishes warnings in a form open to us.

Space 4 sources

Orbits are propagated in the browser from published orbital elements, so satellite positions update every two seconds without any further calls.

SourceWhat it providesWhere it appearsHow we call it
CelesTrakcelestrak.org Orbital elements for seven tracked satellites, plus about 10,700 Starlink satellites Satellite tracker, pass predictions cached
Launch Libraryll.thespacedevs.com Launch schedule, vehicle, mission, pad, launch-weather odds, webcast links Launches tab cached
NASA picture of the dayscience.nasa.gov The daily astronomy photograph, its explanation and credit Bottom of every view cached
Where the ISS atapi.wheretheiss.at Space station orbital elements Backup only, if CelesTrak cannot be reached direct

Sun, moon, planet and eclipse positions are not fetched at all. They are calculated in the page from standard astronomical formulas, accurate to a fraction of a degree.

What this costs and what could break it

Running cost is the virtual machine and nothing else. The risks worth knowing about are these.

RiskWhat happensWhat we do about it
A provider retires an endpoint Happened twice in September 2026. NOAA dropped ten forecast map layers; NASA moved the picture of the day and shut off its old interface. Layers are checked against the provider's live catalogue, and a replacement is wired in. Both outages were fixed the day they were noticed.
Rate limits Launch Library allows an unregistered caller about 15 requests an hour. CelesTrak and NASA throttle too. INMET refuses unfamiliar software and sends 640 KB, mostly icons. Those four are fetched once by our server on a schedule, and trimmed to what the page uses. Viewers read our copy, so the number of visitors does not affect the provider.
A national agency is closed to us Canada and Mexico publish storm warnings, but not in a form we may read. The page links to each agency's own warning page instead of pretending to have the data. Everything else works from coordinates, so it is unaffected.
A provider is briefly unreachable A section would be blank. Each cached source has a chain of fallbacks, ending with the last good copy held in the browser. A tab that genuinely has no data says so rather than showing zeros.
